# Pacing plan: How the Residency Match System Actually Works

Target runtime: **6:20** (inside the 5–7 minute window, with room to grow to 7:00
if the algorithm walkthrough needs it).

Word budgets assume a conversational **150 words per minute** (2.5 words per
second). Recorded voice usually runs a little slower than gTTS, so the preview
will look ~10% short; that is expected.

## Rules of thumb

- **Something changes on screen every 4–8 seconds.** A new arrow, a highlight,
  a camera move or a label counts. A static frame longer than 10 seconds is a
  drop-off risk.
- **A bigger "pattern break" every 30–45 seconds.** A new layout, a zoom, a
  question card, or a color flash. These mark the scene seams below.
- **Ask before you answer.** Each scene opens with the question it resolves, so
  the viewer has a reason to wait for the answer.
- **One open loop is always running.** The hook's question ("how does one
  computer decide this fairly?") is not fully closed until scene 5, and scene 6
  opens a new one ("does it matter who goes first?").

## Scene by scene

### 1. Hook (0:00–0:30)

| Time | Beat | On screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:03 | Cold open, no title card. First line is the stakes: "At noon on Match Day, tens of thousands of medical students find out, at the same moment, where they're going." | Grid of envelopes appears all at once |
| 0:03–0:10 | "Inside is the city they'll live in for the next three to seven years." | One envelope opens; a hospital name lands |
| 0:10–0:20 | The question: "One computer decided all of it. How do you do that fairly?" | Envelopes collapse into a single box labeled "the algorithm" |
| 0:20–0:30 | Promise: "By the end of this video you'll be able to run it by hand." | Short channel title card (under 3 seconds) |

The title card goes **after** the question, never before it. The first frame
must be the envelopes, because it is also the frame YouTube autoplays.

### 2. The problem (0:30–1:15)

| Time | Beat | On screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:30–0:45 | Introduce the four applicants by name, one at a time | Applicant icons slide in, left column |
| 0:45–0:55 | Three programs; one has two slots | Program icons, right column; the 2-slot program gets two seats drawn |
| 0:55–1:10 | Both sides rank each other | Rank lists unfold beside each icon |
| 1:10–1:15 | Question for scene 3: "So who gets matched with who?" | Hold on the full board, 2 seconds max |

### 3. Why naive approaches fail (1:15–2:00)

| Time | Beat | On screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1:15–1:30 | Try an obvious matching | Lines drawn between pairs |
| 1:30–1:45 | Reveal the blocking pair | Both icons and the dashed line flash **red**; everything else dims |
| 1:45–2:00 | History aside: pre-1952 chaos and exploding offers | Quick montage card with a ticking-clock icon; keep it to 15 seconds |

The red flash at about 1:35 is the first big pattern break. It lands right where
viewers who came for the hook start to drift.

### 4. The algorithm (2:00–4:00)

Two minutes is the longest stretch, so it is three beats with a reset between
each.

| Time | Beat | On screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2:00–2:10 | Rules in one sentence: "Propose to your favorite. Programs say maybe to the best so far." | Rule card, then it shrinks to a corner reminder |
| 2:10–2:50 | **Round 1**, slow: every proposal narrated | Blue arrows one at a time, yellow holds, gray rejections |
| 2:50–3:00 | Pattern break: "Now watch what happens when someone better shows up." | Zoom in on the program that is about to bump someone |
| 3:00–3:30 | **Round 2**, a bump narrated in detail | Held applicant turns gray and walks back to the left |
| 3:30–3:50 | **Remaining rounds**, fast-forwarded | 2x speed with a small "fast forward" icon; narration summarizes |
| 3:50–4:00 | Final matching | Everything turns **green** at once, short hold |

The event log drives this, so if the example changes the round count, keep
round 1 and the first bump at full speed and fast-forward the rest.

### 5. Why it's stable (4:00–4:45)

| Time | Beat | On screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4:00–4:10 | Question: "Could there be a blocking pair hiding in here?" | Red dashed line hovers over the green matching |
| 4:10–4:35 | The argument: if A prefers P, A proposed to P earlier and P rejected A for someone better | Replay A's rejection from the log as a quick flashback (desaturated) |
| 4:35–4:45 | Payoff | Red line fades out; "No blocking pairs" check mark |

This closes the hook's loop ("fairly"), so say the word "fair" here on purpose.

### 6. Who proposes matters (4:45–5:30)

| Time | Beat | On screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4:45–4:55 | New question: "What if the hospitals proposed instead?" | The board mirrors: arrows now point right to left |
| 4:55–5:15 | Rerun, fast | Program-proposing run at 2x |
| 5:15–5:30 | Side-by-side reveal, the 1952 twist, then the 1998 switch | Split screen of both matchings, differences highlighted |

The split screen is the second big reveal. Keep both results on screen long
enough to compare (at least 5 seconds of hold with no narration over it).

### 7. Real world (5:30–6:00)

| Time | Beat | On screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5:30–5:45 | Roth–Peranson extension: couples and more | Two linked applicant icons |
| 5:45–6:00 | 2012 Nobel Prize in Economics (Roth and Shapley) | Simple text card |

### 8. Recap and outro (6:00–6:20)

Three recap bullets, then the end screen. No "thanks for watching" preamble;
viewers leave the moment they sense the end, so the recap should be fast and the
end screen should start by 6:10 to leave room for YouTube's 5–20 second end
screen elements.

## Notes for the script and visuals

These are suggestions for whoever writes `narration.md` and `scenes.py`. Facts
come from `sources.md`; anything not confirmed there stays `TODO-VERIFY`.

### Script

1. **Open on a sentence, not a greeting.** No "Hi, welcome back." The first
   words are the stakes. Suggested opener: "At noon on Match Day, tens of
   thousands of medical students find out, at the same moment, where they'll
   spend the next several years." Envelopes are a school tradition, not
   universal, so say "many of them open an envelope" rather than "they all do".
2. **Use the real numbers once, early.** "Over 52,000 applicants and about
   43,000 positions in 2025" makes the problem feel big before the tiny example
   makes it feel solvable. One number set is enough; more turns it into a
   lecture.
3. **Name the four applicants and say the names often.** "Ana proposes to City
   General" is easier to follow than "applicant 1 proposes to program A". Pick
   names that start with different letters and sound different when spoken.
4. **Keep each paragraph to one or two sentences.** `common/narration.py` turns
   each paragraph into one voiceover line, which is one recording take and one
   animation sync point. Short paragraphs mean easier takes and tighter sync.
5. **Turn every scene seam into a question.** End scenes 2, 3, 4 and 5 with the
   question the next scene answers (see the timeline). The viewer stays to hear
   the answer.
6. **Say "fair" in the hook and again in scene 5.** That is the open loop. The
   stability proof is the moment the video keeps its promise, so call back to
   it out loud: "So that's what fair means here."
7. **Save the best history fact for scene 6.** The 1952 Match was, in effect,
   the hospital-proposing version, ten years before Gale and Shapley wrote it
   down. Revealing that right after the side-by-side comparison ("so for 46
   years, the hospitals were getting their best stable outcome") is the
   strongest twist in the video. Then the 1998 switch lands as the fix.
8. **Keep scene 3's history aside to two sentences.** Exploding offers that
   shrank from about 10 days to under 12 hours is vivid; the full timeline
   isn't needed.
9. **Fast-forward lines summarize, they don't narrate.** During the 2x rounds,
   one line like "a few more bumps, and everyone settles" is enough. Narrating
   every event at 2x is where viewers tune out.
10. **No "in this video we will..." agenda.** The promise in the hook ("you'll
    be able to run it by hand") does that job in one line.
11. **End fast.** The recap is three short bullets, then one forward-looking
    line about the next video. No "thanks for watching, don't forget to...".

### Visuals

1. **First frame is the envelope grid**, already on screen at frame 0 with no
   fade from black. YouTube's autoplay preview and the first second of the
   video are the same frame, and a black frame reads as "nothing here".
2. **One new thing every 4–8 seconds.** If a narration line runs longer than 8
   seconds over a static board, add a small change: highlight the rank list
   entry being read, pulse the icon being talked about, or nudge the camera.
3. **Dim everything that isn't the subject.** When one proposal or one bump is
   the focus, fade the other icons to about 40% opacity. It tells the eye where
   to look, which matters more on phones.
4. **The red blocking-pair flash is the only red in the first two minutes.**
   Keep red reserved so it stays a shock. Same for the green final state: it
   should not appear until 3:50.
5. **Zoom on the first bump only.** The camera zoom in `AlgorithmScene` before
   the first bump is the right pattern break. Don't repeat the zoom on later
   bumps, or it stops feeling special.
6. **Fast-forward badge plus speed.** The existing badge is good. Pair it with a
   visibly faster `run_time` (about half the round-1 step time) so it reads as
   fast without the badge.
7. **Flashback style for scene 5.** Replaying the rejection from the event log
   in a desaturated or slightly transparent style signals "this already
   happened" without extra narration.
8. **Mirror, don't rebuild, for scene 6.** Keep the same board and flip the
   arrows to point right to left. Viewers already know the layout, so the only
   new information is the direction, which makes the different result jump out.
9. **Hold the split screen 5 seconds with no narration.** Silence over a
   comparison is a pattern break of its own and gives the viewer time to spot
   the difference before it's explained.
10. **On-screen text stays at 6 words or fewer per card**, at `BODY_SIZE` or
    larger, so it's readable on a phone without pausing.
11. **No channel or presenter branding beyond the shared title card.** No
    names, faces or third-party logos on screen; the NRMP is named in narration
    only, as a fact.

## Packaging

The title and thumbnail should promise the same thing the first 30 seconds
deliver: a high-stakes moment and a "how does that even work?" question.
Neither should repeat the other; the thumbnail shows the moment, the title asks
the question.

### Title options

Keep it under about 60 characters so it isn't cut off on phones.

| # | Title | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **How the Residency Match Actually Works** | The working title, trimmed. Clear and searchable; "actually" promises the real mechanism. Recommended. |
| 2 | One Algorithm Decides Where Every New Doctor Goes | Bigger stakes, more curiosity. Slightly less searchable. |
| 3 | The Algorithm That Matches 40,000 Doctors a Year | A concrete number is a strong hook. `sources.md` has 43,237 positions in 2025, so "40,000" is a safe round-down. |
| 4 | Why Med Students Can't Game the Match | Leads with the strategy-proof payoff. Good as a second-video or Shorts angle. |
| 5 | Gale-Shapley Explained: The Residency Match | Best for search from CS students; weakest for a general audience. |

Recommendation: title 1 at launch. If click-through is low after a week, swap
to title 2 or 3 (YouTube's title A/B test feature can do this for you).

### Thumbnail concepts

All three use the channel palette from `common/style.py`, at most three words of
text, and no faces, names or third-party logos.

1. **The envelope (recommended).** A single envelope, half open, on the deep
   azure background, with a glowing tangerine card sliding out that shows a
   hospital icon and a big "?". Text: "WHO DECIDES?" It matches the first frame
   of the video, so the click feels like it delivered.
2. **The blocking pair.** The board from scene 3: four applicant icons, three
   hospital icons, one red dashed line between an applicant and a hospital,
   everything else dimmed. Text: "THIS BREAKS IT". More "nerdy", good for a CS
   audience.
3. **Two answers.** A split frame, same people on both sides, different lines
   drawn, a big "≠" in the middle. Text: "WHO ASKS FIRST?" Teases the scene 6
   twist.

The thumbnail can be rendered from a Manim frame (`scene.renderer.get_frame()`
or `manim -s`) so it uses the real board, then text added at 1280x720.
